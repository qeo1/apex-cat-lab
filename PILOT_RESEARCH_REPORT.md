### APX-QA-001 · QA

**Original question**

How many ordered triples of nonnegative integers (a,b,c) satisfy a+b+c=12, a≤4, b is even, and c≥1?

**Choices**

- A. 22
- B. 24
- C. 26
- D. 28

**Canonical answer:** 26

**CAT PYQ benchmark:** CAT 2025 Slot 2 QA Q14, GradeScale — [https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/2/view/](https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/2/view/)

**Benchmark fingerprint:** Count exponent tuples satisfying a modular residue condition.

**Research source:** GLOB-11 — [https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/video_galleries/lecture-videos/](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/video_galleries/lecture-videos/)

**Research insight:** The course explicitly develops inclusion–exclusion, counting, generating functions, modular arithmetic and linear programming. We adapt the reasoning operators to self-contained CAT-scale arithmetic, not the lecture problems.

**Extracted reasoning structure:** bounded integer compositions / coefficient counting

**Novel transformation:** Replaces prime-exponent residue filtering with a bounded composition and a parity restriction; the sample space is generated by two bounded choices and a slack variable, not divisor factorization.

**Progressive hints**

1. Set c′=c−1 so the remaining total is 11.
2. Write b=2k and sum over feasible k.
3. For k=0,…,5, count a from 0 through min(4,11−2k).

**Independent re-solve:** Enumerating k=0,…,5 gives respectively 5,5,5,5,4,2 admissible a-values; each determines one c′, so the total is 26.

**Expert solution:** Let c′=c−1≥0. Then a+2k+c′=11 for b=2k. For each k=0,…,5, a can range from 0 to min(4,11−2k), while c′ is then fixed. The counts are 5,5,5,5,4,2, totaling 26.

**Adversarial ambiguity and uniqueness check:** Ordered triples are explicit; zero is permitted for a and b; c≥1 is converted exactly; k range ends at 5. Each counted (a,k) gives one c′, so no overcount.

**CAT relevance:** Tests finite counting and disciplined case enumeration, useful quantitative reasoning; generating functions are a design source, not required knowledge.

**CAT difficulty:** Moderate

**Training difficulty:** Moderate

### APX-QA-002 · QA

**Original question**

A screening system flags 80% of members of a group and 10% of nonmembers. In a population, 5% are members. Given that a person is flagged, what is the probability that the person is a member?

**Choices**

- A. 8/27
- B. 4/9
- C. 8/19
- D. 1/5

**Canonical answer:** 8/27

**CAT PYQ benchmark:** CAT 2025 Slot 2 QA Q2, GradeScale — [https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/2/view/](https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/2/view/)

**Benchmark fingerprint:** Recover a missing observation from overlapping means and a relation between adjacent data.

**Research source:** GLOB-12 — [https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/pages/syllabus/](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/pages/syllabus/)

**Research insight:** The reviewed syllabus treats conditional probability, Bayes’ theorem, base rates, expectation and independence as distinct ideas. Pilot probability items state base rates and sampling assumptions explicitly.

**Extracted reasoning structure:** Bayesian base-rate update

**Novel transformation:** Changes overlapping-average recovery into a base-rate update. It requires conditioning on the observed flag and retaining both flagged groups in the denominator.

**Progressive hints**

1. Imagine 1000 people to avoid decimals.
2. Count flagged members and flagged nonmembers separately.
3. Divide flagged members by all flagged people.

**Independent re-solve:** Using Bayes’ rule: (0.8×0.05)/[(0.8×0.05)+(0.1×0.95)]=0.04/0.135=8/27.

**Expert solution:** Among 1000 people, 50 are members and 950 are not. The system flags 40 members and 95 nonmembers. Thus P(member|flagged)=40/(40+95)=40/135=8/27.

**Adversarial ambiguity and uniqueness check:** Sensitivity and false-positive rate are defined in the prompt; population prevalence is explicit; assume the stated rates apply uniformly. Denominator includes both flagged categories.

**CAT relevance:** Strengthens conditional reasoning and careful denominator selection; the CAT syllabus status of advanced probability topics is not asserted.

**CAT difficulty:** Moderate

**Training difficulty:** Moderate

**Validation state:** approved — Blind deterministic check passed.

### APX-QA-003 · QA

**Original question**

A workshop makes stools (s) and benches (b), both nonnegative whole numbers. Each stool uses 2 units of timber and 1 hour; each bench uses 1 unit of timber and 2 hours. At most 14 timber units and 16 hours are available. Profit is 5 credits per stool and 6 per bench. What is the maximum profit?

**Choices**

- B. 54
- C. 56
- D. 58

**Canonical answer:** 56 credits

**CAT PYQ benchmark:** CAT 2025 Slot 3 QA Q3, GradeScale — [https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/3/view/](https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/3/view/)

**Benchmark fingerprint:** Maximize one student group under a linear budget equation and a dominance constraint.

**Research insight:** MIT 18.200 includes linear programming and duality. The pilot uses only feasible-region constraints and small integer enumeration, not advanced algorithms.

**Extracted reasoning structure:** two-constraint integer optimization

**Novel transformation:** Transforms a demographic count-maximization constraint into a two-product integer resource model with two capacities and a profit objective; it does not reuse the CAT item’s population-total and fee equation.

**Progressive hints**

1. Write 2s+b≤14 and s+2b≤16.
2. The continuous boundary intersection is a useful candidate.
3. Check nearby integer pairs and axis endpoints.

**Independent re-solve:** Enumerating s=0,…,7 and taking b≤min(14−2s, floor((16−s)/2)) gives best profits 48,47,52,51,56,49,42,35. The maximum is 56 at (4,6).

**Expert solution:** At the intersection, 2s+b=14 and s+2b=16, giving s=4,b=6 and profit 56. For each integer s=0,…,7, the feasible maximum b is min(14−2s, floor((16−s)/2)); the corresponding best profits are 48, 47, 52, 51, 56, 49, 42, 35. Thus 56 is the integer maximum.

**Adversarial ambiguity and uniqueness check:** Whole-number production and inclusive resource caps are explicit; profit is linear with no setup costs. Enumeration covers every feasible s since 2s≤14.

**CAT relevance:** Applies equations, inequalities and integer feasibility to a short optimization model; no advanced linear-programming algorithm is required.

### APX-QA-009 · QA

**Original question**

A right triangle has perpendicular sides of lengths 6 and 8. A circle is centered at the right-angle vertex and tangent to the line containing the hypotenuse. What is the circle’s area?

**Choices**

- A. 144π/25
- B. 288π/25
- C. 576π/25
- D. 576π/5

**Canonical answer:** 576π/25

**CAT PYQ benchmark:** CAT 2025 Slot 2 QA Q16, GradeScale — [https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/2/view/](https://www.gradscale.in/pyqs/cat/2025/2/view/)

**Benchmark fingerprint:** Area ratio in a regular-hexagon construction with a trapezium; benchmark is a geometry/area task.

**Research source:** GLOB-15 — [https://people.csail.mit.edu/sukhaj/ARMLBook/TrianglesF.pdf](https://people.csail.mit.edu/sukhaj/ARMLBook/TrianglesF.pdf)

**Research insight:** The reviewed triangle reference states area as one half base times perpendicular height and gives right-triangle relationships. The question uses the altitude to the hypotenuse as a point-to-line distance; no contest exercise is reused.

**Extracted reasoning structure:** right-triangle altitude as point-to-line distance

**Novel transformation:** The CAT benchmark compares areas in a regular-hexagon/trapezium construction. This item uses a perpendicular altitude as the radius of a circle centered at a vertex, changing both the geometric representation and target quantity.

**Progressive hints**

1. Find the hypotenuse using the Pythagorean theorem.
2. Use the triangle’s area to get the perpendicular height to that hypotenuse.
3. That height is the circle’s radius; square it and multiply by π.

**Independent re-solve:** The perpendicular distance from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse is ab/c=(6·8)/10=24/5. A circle tangent to that line and centered at the vertex has this radius; its area is 576π/25.

**Expert solution:** The hypotenuse is 10. Triangle area is (1/2)(6)(8)=24. If h is the altitude to the hypotenuse, (1/2)(10)h=24, so h=24/5. The circle’s radius is the distance from its center to the tangent line, hence r=24/5. Its area is πr²=576π/25.

**Adversarial ambiguity and uniqueness check:** The prompt says tangent to the line containing the hypotenuse, so the radius is the unique perpendicular distance to that line; no assumption that the full circle lies inside the triangle is needed.

**CAT relevance:** Combines Pythagorean geometry, triangle area and perpendicular distance; it is a geometry reasoning exercise, not an exam-frequency prediction.

**Progressive hints**

### APX-VARC-021 · VARC

**Shared set — VARC · When a map becomes a decision**

A city’s heat map is often described as if it simply discovers where heat is. In practice, it is assembled from choices: which surfaces to measure, at what hour, at what height, and at what scale to average them. A satellite may register a hot roof at noon while missing the shaded bus stop where people wait at dusk. A block-level average can make two neighbourhoods look alike even when one has tree-lined courtyards and the other has crowded rooms that retain heat overnight.

This does not make the map useless. A model can reveal a pattern too broad or slow for any one resident to see. When several measures point to the same district, the map can help a city decide where to inspect first. Its value is especially clear when it directs scarce crews toward places that would otherwise receive little attention. The error begins when a first-pass signal is treated as a final verdict.

Every map also carries a warrant: an assumption that the measured feature is a good stand-in for the condition the city cares about. Surface temperature might stand in for risk to residents; tree canopy might stand in for access to shade. Such links may be reasonable, but neither is identical to the thing it represents. A canopy percentage says little about whether shade falls over a pavement at the hour it is needed.

A sensible response is not to abandon measurement or to demand a perfect map. It is to give the map a defined job. Use it to select sites for closer observation, publish the scale and timing of its measurements, and invite residents to identify conditions the sensors miss. Where the model and local accounts diverge, that disagreement is information about what the model leaves out.

The practical question is therefore not whether a map is objective or subjective. ### APX-VARC-024 · VARC

**Shared set — VARC · When a map becomes a decision**

A city’s heat map is often described as if it simply discovers where heat is. In practice, it is assembled from choices: which surfaces to measure, at what hour, at what height, and at what scale to average them. A satellite may register a hot roof at noon while missing the shaded bus stop where people wait at dusk. A block-level average can make two neighbourhoods look alike even when one has tree-lined courtyards and the other has crowded rooms that retain heat overnight.

This does not make the map useless. A model can reveal a pattern too broad or slow for any one resident to see. When several measures point to the same district, the map can help a city decide where to inspect first. Its value is especially clear when it directs scarce crews toward places that would otherwise receive little attention. The error begins when a first-pass signal is treated as a final verdict.

Every map also carries a warrant: an assumption that the measured feature is a good stand-in for the condition the city cares about. Surface temperature might stand in for risk to residents; tree canopy might stand in for access to shade. Such links may be reasonable, but neither is identical to the thing it represents. A canopy percentage says little about whether shade falls over a pavement at the hour it is needed.

A sensible response is not to abandon measurement or to demand a perfect map. It is to give the map a defined job. Use it to select sites for closer observation, publish the scale and timing of its measurements, and invite residents to identify conditions the sensors miss. Where the model and local accounts diverge, that disagreement is information about what the model leaves out.

The practical question is therefore not whether a map is objective or subjective. It is whether its assumptions are visible, whether its limits are matched to its use, and whether the people affected can challenge the categories through which resources are assigned. A map can support fairer decisions, but only if those decisions remain revisable when better evidence arrives.
